Section 3Definitions and classification

Heart failure is a clinical syndrome of symptoms and/or signs caused by structural or functional cardiac abnormality, producing raised intracardiac pressures or inadequate cardiac output at rest or on exertion, confirmed by objective evidence of congestion or supportive testing.

Phenotypes by ejection fraction

HFrEFLVEF < 50% with symptoms and/or signs of HFLVEF ≤ 40%

HFpEFLVEF ≥ 50% with symptoms and/or signs, plus objective evidence of diastolic dysfunction or raised filling pressures, supported by raised natriuretic peptidesLVEF ≥ 50%

The rationale is pathophysiological: patients in the old mid-range behave like HFrEF in aetiology, contractility, outcomes and treatment response, and many of them deteriorate further. Remember that any single LVEF cut-point is partly arbitrary and can read falsely high — in mitral regurgitation, for example.

Stages, from risk to advanced disease

Stage A — at risk

Risk factors without structural disease or symptoms. Not everyone progresses, but intervention is often warranted.

Stage B — pre-HF

Structural or functional abnormality — hypertrophy, chamber enlargement, wall motion or tissue abnormality, valve disease, raised filling pressures — without symptoms.

Stage C — symptomatic HF

Current or previous symptoms and/or signs. This is where the phenotypes apply.

Stage D — advanced HF

Severe symptoms and dysfunction despite full therapy, with recurrent decompensation. See Section 8.

Section 4Prevention of heart failure

Stage A is about risk factors; stage B is structural or functional heart disease before symptoms. Both are treatable targets.

Stage A — patients at risk

  • IA

    Blood pressure control to a systolic target below 130 mmHg in hypertension or stage B HF

  • IA

    SGLT2 inhibitor in type 2 diabetes at risk of HF (multiple ASCVD risk factors or established ASCVD)

  • IA

    SGLT2 inhibitor in type 2 diabetes with chronic kidney disease

  • IA

    Finerenone in type 2 diabetes with chronic kidney disease

  • IC

    Lifestyle counselling for every stage A or B patient — weight, balanced diet, activity, no smoking, no heavy alcohol, no cocaine / amphetamines / anabolic steroids

  • IIaA

    Statin in people at high risk of, or with established, ASCVD

  • IIaA

    GLP-1 receptor agonist in type 2 diabetes plus at least one other CV risk factor

Stage B — structural disease, no symptoms

  • IA

    ACE inhibitor (or ARB if ACE-I not tolerated) when LVEF ≤ 40%

  • IB1

    Beta-blocker when LVEF < 50%

  • IB1

    Eplerenone after myocardial infarction with LVEF ≤ 40% plus signs of HF or diabetes

Section 5Diagnosis of chronic heart failure

Natriuretic peptides plus echocardiography carry the diagnosis. Everything else answers the next question: which phenotype, and which aetiology.

First-line work-up in suspected HF

  • IC

    Screening bloods: full blood count, eGFR and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, electrolytes, liver and thyroid function, HbA1c, lipids, iron status (TSAT and ferritin)

  • IC

    Natriuretic peptide measurement, interpreted against age, obesity and other modifiers

  • IC

    12-lead ECG

  • IC

    Chest radiograph

  • IC

    Transthoracic echocardiogram to confirm the diagnosis, separate phenotypes and point to aetiology

Looking for a reversible or treatable cause

  • IC

    Contrast-enhanced CMR when cardiomyopathy is suspected or the aetiology is uncertain and characterization would change care

  • IB

    Serum and urine immunofixation, serum free light chains and bone scintigraphy (DPD/PYP/HMDP) when cardiac amyloidosis is suspected

  • IC

    Genetic testing when cardiomyopathy criteria are met and it changes diagnosis, prognosis, therapy, reproductive planning, or allows cascade screening of relatives

  • IC

    Invasive coronary angiography in HF with angina and high probability of obstructive CAD who could be revascularized

  • IIaC

    18F-FDG-PET when cardiac sarcoidosis is suspected

  • IIaC

    Endomyocardial biopsy in rapidly progressive HF despite therapy, or when inflammation, infiltration or storage is suspected and cannot be shown otherwise

  • IIaC

    Right heart catheterization when constrictive pericarditis or congenital disease is the suspected cause

  • IIbC

    Right heart catheterization in selected suspected HFpEF or structural disease when non-invasive testing leaves the haemodynamics unclear

In established HFrEF

  • IIaC

    Invasive coronary angiography when pre-test likelihood of obstructive CAD is high, weighing procedural risk against a realistic revascularization plan

  • IIaC

    CT coronary angiography when pre-test likelihood is low to moderate

  • IIaC

    Review the proportion of RV pacing in every patient with a device and new or worsening HFrEF

  • IIbC

    Electrical cardioversion as a diagnostic step in persistent AF when the value of sinus rhythm on symptoms or LV function is uncertain

Natriuretic peptide thresholds

Outpatient — HF likely above

AgeNT-proBNP
Under 50 years≥ 125 pg/mL
50–75 years≥ 250 pg/mL
Over 75 years≥ 500 pg/mL

Age-adjusted thresholds replace the single 125 pg/mL rule-out; over 75% of healthy people above 80 exceed the old cut-off. There is not yet enough data for age-specific BNP cut-offs.

Decompensated HF — rule out below

  • NT-proBNP < 300 pg/mL
  • BNP < 100 ng/mL
  • MR-proANP < 120 pg/mL

Age-related rule-in values: NT-proBNP > 450 pg/mL under 50 years, > 900 pg/mL at 50–74 years, > 1800 pg/mL over 74 years. NT-proBNP above 2000 pg/mL in primary care doubles the risk of early hospitalization — see the patient quickly.

Peptides rise with age, AF, kidney dysfunction, LV hypertrophy, myocarditis, pulmonary embolism or hypertension, critical illness and RV pacing. They fall with obesity, African ancestry and treatment with ARNI, ACE-I or ARB — read every value against the patient in front of you.

Section 6.1Drug therapy for chronic heart failure

Two drugs now sit under every phenotype. SGLT2 inhibitors and MRAs are foundational whatever the ejection fraction; the rest of the foundation is added in HFrEF.

All patients with HF, independent of LVEF

  • IA

    SGLT2 inhibitor (dapagliflozin or empagliflozin) in symptomatic HF at any LVEF

  • IA

    MRA in symptomatic HF at any LVEF — steroidal in HFrEF, steroidal or non-steroidal in HFpEF

  • IA

    Loop diuretic dosed dynamically to volume status when there are signs or symptoms of congestion

  • IC

    Uptitrate foundational therapy at least every 1–2 weeks, guided by symptoms, vital signs and labs, towards trial target doses

  • IC

    Continue foundational therapy at the highest tolerated dose, including patients who become asymptomatic or whose LVEF improves

HFrEF (LVEF < 50%)

  • IA

    Beta-blocker in stable symptomatic HFrEF

  • IA

    ACE inhibitor or ARNI in symptomatic HFrEF

  • IB1

    Switch from ACE inhibitor or ARB to ARNI in symptomatic HFrEF

  • IA

    ARB when ACE inhibitor and ARNI are both not tolerated

  • IIaB1

    Cardiac glycoside (digoxin or digitoxin) with LVEF ≤ 40% despite optimal foundational therapy

  • IIaB1

    Ivabradine with LVEF ≤ 35%, sinus rhythm, heart rate > 70 b.p.m. on maximally tolerated foundational therapy

  • IIaC

    Ivabradine with LVEF ≤ 35% in sinus rhythm when a beta-blocker cannot be tolerated

  • IIaB2

    Hydralazine plus isosorbide dinitrate in self-identified black patients with LVEF ≤ 40% on optimal therapy

  • IIbC

    Hydralazine plus isosorbide dinitrate when ARNI, ACE inhibitor and ARB are all not tolerated

  • IIbB1

    Vericiguat with LVEF < 45% despite optimal foundational therapy

  • IIbC

    Gradual withdrawal of foundational therapy only in highly selected asymptomatic patients with fully normalized function, volumes and natriuretic peptides after a reversible cause was treated — under close surveillance

HFpEF (LVEF ≥ 50%)

  • IIbC

    ACE inhibitor, ARB or ARNI may be added in symptomatic HFpEF to reduce hospitalization

Section 6.2Devices and interventional therapy

Guideline-directed interventional therapy (GDIT) covers every device and procedure with a recommendation — it works with the drugs, not after them.

Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator

  • IA

    Secondary prevention after a haemodynamically unstable ventricular arrhythmia, no reversible cause, survival > 1 year with good functional status expected

  • IB1

    Primary prevention in symptomatic HFrEF of ischaemic aetiology (not within 40 days of MI), LVEF ≤ 35% after ≥ 3 months of optimal foundational therapy

  • IIaB1

    Primary prevention in non-ischaemic aetiology, LVEF ≤ 35% after ≥ 3 months of optimal foundational therapy

  • IIaC

    Shared decision-making with an experienced cardiologist before generator replacement

  • IIbC

    Wearable cardioverter-defibrillator for a limited period as a bridge to decision or to transplantation

  • IIIB1

    Not for primary prevention within 40 days of myocardial infarction

  • IIIC

    Not in NYHA class IV refractory to drug therapy unless a candidate for CRT, assist device or transplantation

Cardiac resynchronization therapy

  • IA

    Symptomatic HFrEF, LVEF ≤ 35%, sinus rhythm, LBBB with QRS ≥ 150 ms despite optimal foundational therapy

  • IIaB1

    Upgrade to CRT with LVEF ≤ 35% and worsening HF on a pacemaker or ICD with a high proportion of RV pacing

  • IIaB1

    CRT rather than RV pacing in HFrEF with an indication for pacing for high-degree AV block, any NYHA class or QRS width

  • IIaC

    Non-LBBB with QRS ≥ 150 ms, LVEF ≤ 35%, sinus rhythm

  • IIaC

    LBBB with QRS 130–149 ms, LVEF ≤ 35%, sinus rhythm

  • IIbC

    Non-LBBB with QRS 130–149 ms, LVEF ≤ 35%, sinus rhythm

  • IIbC

    Starting foundational therapy and planning CRT at the same time in LBBB ≥ 150 ms with LVEF ≤ 35% — reassess LVEF before implantation

  • IIIA

    Not indicated with QRS < 130 ms in the absence of a pacing indication

Section 7Decompensated heart failure

Acute HF is now called decompensated HF: new or gradually worsening symptoms severe enough to force an unplanned admission, emergency visit or urgent ambulatory visit.

Oxygen and ventilation

  • IC

    Oxygen when SpO₂ < 90% or PaO₂ < 60 mmHg

  • IC

    Intubation for persistent, progressive respiratory failure despite oxygen or non-invasive ventilation

  • IIaB2

    Non-invasive positive pressure ventilation in respiratory distress (rate > 25/min, SpO₂ < 90%), started as early as possible

Decongestion

  • IA

    Intravenous loop diuretic for every patient admitted with fluid overload

  • IIaB1

    Add short-term intravenous acetazolamide or oral hydrochlorothiazide when fluid overload persists on loop diuretics

  • IIbB1

    Urinary sodium-guided diuretic dosing during the first days of treatment

  • IB1

    Start an SGLT2 inhibitor in hospital once the patient is stabilized

Vasoactive drugs

  • IIbC

    Intravenous vasodilators as initial therapy when systolic BP > 110 mmHg

  • IIbC

    Inotropes when systolic BP < 90 mmHg with hypoperfusion not responding to standard treatment including a fluid challenge

  • IIbC

    Vasopressors, norepinephrine preferred, in cardiogenic shock

  • IIIC

    Routine opiates are not recommended unless there is severe pain or anxiety

Temporary mechanical circulatory support in cardiogenic shock

  • IC

    A multidisciplinary Shock Team decides device modality and type in potential candidates

  • IIaB1

    Microaxial flow pump in selected STEMI-related shock with LV systolic dysfunction and no risk of hypoxic brain injury

  • IIaC

    Temporary support for MI-related mechanical complications as a bridge to definitive treatment

  • IIaC

    Temporary support in selected HF-related haemodynamic instability as a bridge to recovery, decision, bridge, candidacy or transplantation

  • IIIB1

    Not for unselected cardiogenic shock after acute MI — risk of harm

  • IIIB1

    Intra-aortic balloon pump not recommended in unselected cardiogenic shock — no effect

Before and after discharge

  • IC

    Careful evaluation before discharge to exclude persisting congestion

  • IB2

    Rapid initiation and uptitration of foundational therapy before discharge, with frequent visits over the first 6 weeks

Reading the presentation

Left-sided congestion

  • Dyspnoea, orthopnoea, cough, tachypnoea
  • Rales, third heart sound
  • Pleural effusion
  • Raised natriuretic peptides

Right-sided congestion

  • Peripheral oedema, abdominal distension, ascites
  • Hepatomegaly, jugular venous distension, hepatojugular reflux
  • Raised creatinine, bilirubin, ALP and GGT
  • Only slightly raised natriuretic peptides

Hypoperfusion

  • Cold sweaty extremities, pale skin
  • Dizziness, confusion, oliguria
  • Narrow pulse pressure
  • Raised lactate, creatinine and aminotransferases

Section 8Advanced heart failure

The message is timing. Refer while the patient still has options, not when end-organ failure has closed them.

Recognition and evaluation

  • IA

    Early consultation with an advanced HF centre for patients with, or at risk of, advanced HF who are motivated and have no absolute contraindication to transplantation or durable support

  • IC

    Cardiopulmonary exercise testing as part of transplantation and durable support evaluation

  • IC

    Right heart catheterization as part of transplantation and durable support evaluation

Medical management

  • IIaC

    Down-titrate or stop beta-blockers or ivabradine in selected patients with organ hypoperfusion despite initial treatment

  • IIaC

    Continuous inotropes with low cardiac output and hypoperfusion or end-organ dysfunction, as a bridge to decision, transplantation or durable support

  • IIaC

    Kidney replacement therapy in refractory volume overload with end-stage kidney failure

  • IIbC

    Ultrafiltration in refractory volume overload unresponsive to diuretic escalation

Durable support and transplantation

  • IC

    Durable LVAD in selected advanced HFrEF despite foundational and interventional therapy — as bridge to transplantation, candidacy, recovery, or as destination therapy

  • IIaC

    Histopathology of explanted heart tissue after LVAD or transplantation in non-ischaemic HF, to find a treatable cause or guide family screening

  • IC

    Heart transplantation in selected advanced HF refractory to foundational and interventional therapy without contraindications

Defining advanced heart failure

All of these, despite foundational, additional and interventional therapy

  • Severe, persistent symptoms — NYHA class III or IV.
  • Severe cardiac dysfunction: LVEF ≤ 30%; or isolated RV failure; or inoperable severe valve or congenital lesions; or persistently high natriuretic peptides with severe diastolic dysfunction or structural abnormality.
  • Episodes of congestion needing high-dose intravenous diuretics, or malignant arrhythmias, causing ≥ 2 unplanned visits or admissions in 12 months.
  • Severe impairment of exercise capacity of cardiac origin.

Rule of three — at least one

  • ≥ 2 HF admissions, or ≥ 1 needing inotropes, in the last year
  • Diuretic resistance
  • Intolerance of neurohormonal drugs with cardiorenal syndrome

I NEED HELP — at least one

  • Inotropes
  • NYHA III/IV or persistently high natriuretic peptides
  • End-organ dysfunction (liver, kidney)
  • EF < 20% or poor RV function
  • Defibrillator shocks
  • More than one HF admission
  • Escalating diuretics
  • Low blood pressure with high heart rate
  • Intolerance of prognostic drugs, or need to down-titrate
Contraindications to heart transplantation

Absolute: inability to comply with treatment (for example dementia); serious comorbidity with poor prognosis that transplantation will not reverse.

Other: active infection; severe peripheral or cerebrovascular disease; pharmacologically irreversible pulmonary vascular resistance; cancer; irreversible liver cirrhosis or kidney dysfunction; multiorgan systemic disease; BMI > 35 kg/m² or severe cachexia; alcohol or substance misuse or active smoking within 6 months; psychological instability; social support insufficient for outpatient care.

Section 9Cardiovascular comorbidities

Atrial fibrillation, coronary disease and valve disease decide much of the prognosis — and each has a changed recommendation this cycle.

Atrial fibrillation

  • IA

    Oral anticoagulation when CHA₂DS₂-VA indicates elevated thromboembolic risk

  • IB1

    Direct oral anticoagulants preferred over vitamin K antagonists

  • IC

    Beta-blockers as first-line rate control in stable HFrEF with AF

  • IIaC

    Digoxin when rate stays high on beta-blockers, or when beta-blockers are contraindicated or not tolerated

  • IIaB1

    AV node ablation with CRT in severely symptomatic permanent AF with poor rate control despite drugs and at least one HF hospitalization

  • IIbC

    Intravenous amiodarone or digoxin in haemodynamically unstable HFrEF with AF for acute rate control

  • IC

    Urgent cardioversion in decompensated HF with rapid ventricular rate and haemodynamic instability

  • IIaC

    Catheter ablation in selected patients with symptomatic AF and HFrEF

Chronic coronary syndrome

  • IC

    Revascularization in HF with obstructive CAD and persistent angina despite foundational and antianginal therapy

  • IIaB1

    CABG as the preferred strategy, after Heart Team assessment, with LVEF ≤ 35% and multivessel disease suitable for surgery

Valvular heart disease

  • IB1

    Aortic valve intervention (SAVR or TAVI) in severe aortic stenosis with HF

  • IB1

    Mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair in stable symptomatic HFrEF with persistent severe secondary mitral regurgitation despite optimized therapy and CRT, meeting the selection criteria

  • IIbC

    Mitral TEER in selected symptomatic patients who do not meet all criteria, after LVAD and transplantation have been excluded

A score of 2 or more is recommended as the trigger for oral anticoagulation; a score of 1 should be considered a trigger. Lenient rate control — resting rate below 110 b.p.m. — is the initial target, then reassess by symptoms. Catheter ablation is for selected patients: high AF burden, persistent AF for less than a year, and a clear cause-and-effect link between AF and HF.

Who qualifies for mitral edge-to-edge repair

  • Optimized foundational therapy and CRT if indicated
  • NYHA class ≥ II
  • An HF admission in the last year, or BNP ≥ 300 pg/mL or NT-proBNP ≥ 1000 pg/mL
  • Not stage D (advanced) HF
  • No CAD needing revascularization
  • No hypertrophic, restrictive or infiltrative cardiomyopathy
  • LVEF 20–50%, LV end-systolic diameter ≤ 70 mm
  • Anatomy suitable for TEER; systolic PA pressure ≤ 70 mmHg
  • No severe RV dysfunction; no severe aortic or tricuspid disease

Section 10Non-cardiovascular comorbidities

Obesity treatment enters the guideline with an incretin recommendation; iron, lung disease, mood and frailty complete the screen.

Obesity

  • IIaB1

    Semaglutide or tirzepatide in symptomatic HF with LVEF ≥ 45% and BMI ≥ 30 kg/m², whether or not the patient has diabetes

  • IIbC

    Bariatric surgery with BMI ≥ 35 kg/m² when structured lifestyle change plus weight-reducing drugs fail to maintain weight loss

Iron deficiency

  • IB1

    Intravenous iron in symptomatic HFrEF with iron deficiency, to relieve symptoms and improve quality of life

  • IIaB1

    Intravenous iron in symptomatic HFrEF with iron deficiency, to reduce hospitalization

Lung disease and sleep-disordered breathing

  • IIbC

    Adaptive servo-ventilation may be considered in HFrEF with predominantly obstructive sleep apnoea

  • IIIA

    Adaptive servo-ventilation is not recommended in HFrEF with predominantly central sleep apnoea — increased mortality

Mood, frailty and general care

  • IIaC

    Assess anxiety, depression and frailty to shape a personalized care plan

  • IIaC

    Influenza and pneumococcal vaccination

  • IIIB2

    NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors are not recommended — they worsen HF and drive hospitalization

Section 11Team management, rehabilitation and follow-up

Exercise-based rehabilitation moves up to a Class I recommendation, and palliative care is written into the advanced-stage pathway.

Multidisciplinary management

  • IB1

    A multidisciplinary HF management programme

  • IA

    HF education and self-management strategies

  • IB2

    Interventions to improve adherence, including health-literacy assessment and polypharmacy review

Exercise training and rehabilitation

  • IB1

    Personalized exercise training within multidisciplinary exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation for all stable patients without contraindications

  • IB2

    Personalized exercise training outside formal rehabilitation, long term, for all stable patients without contraindications

  • IB1

    Cardiac rehabilitation after LVAD implantation

  • IIaB1

    Cardiac rehabilitation after decompensated HF, as soon as it is safe

Telemonitoring and long-term follow-up

  • IIaB1

    Remote pulmonary artery pressure monitoring in symptomatic NYHA class III patients with an HF hospitalization in the past 12 months

  • IIbB1

    Non-invasive telemonitoring may be considered to reduce hospitalization

  • IB1

    Proactive discussion of trajectory, goals of care and advance care planning in advanced HF

  • IB2

    Access to an integrated HF palliative care team at the advanced stage

Section 12Specific conditions

Pregnancy and cardiac amyloidosis carry the two most practice-changing statements of this section.

Pregnancy

  • IC

    Pre-conception care and counselling covering HF treatment, pregnancy, contraception, pre-implantation genetic screening and assisted reproduction

  • IC

    In pregnant women with HFrEF, switch non-selective beta-blockers to beta1-selective agents (metoprolol, bisoprolol) with close maternal and foetal monitoring

  • IIIC

    ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs, MRAs, ivabradine and SGLT2 inhibitors are not recommended in pregnancy — foetotoxicity and teratogenicity

Cardiac amyloidosis

  • IA

    A TTR silencer (vutrisiran) or stabilizer (tafamidis or acoramidis) in variant or wild-type transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis, NYHA class I–III

Section 13Key messages

The Task Force's own summary of what should change in practice.

Diagnosis

  • Natriuretic peptides and echocardiography carry the diagnosis; further tests define aetiology and phenotype.
  • Use multimodality assessment to find treatable causes.

Therapy

  • ARNI or ACE-I (ARB if intolerant), beta-blocker, MRA and SGLT2-I are foundational in HFrEF; MRA and SGLT2-I are foundational in HFpEF.
  • ICD is recommended in selected HFrEF with LVEF ≤ 35% of ischaemic aetiology, and should be considered in non-ischaemic aetiology.
  • CRT-P or CRT-D is recommended with LVEF ≤ 35%, sinus rhythm and LBBB ≥ 150 ms, and should be considered with LBBB 130–149 ms or non-LBBB ≥ 150 ms.

Decompensated heart failure

  • Treat the trigger urgently; diuretics for congestion, vasodilators when blood pressure is high, inotropes or temporary support for hypoperfusion.
  • Exclude residual congestion before discharge, optimize foundational therapy before the patient leaves, and plan close early follow-up.

Advanced disease and comorbidity

  • Refer patients refractory to therapy for transplantation or mechanical support while options remain open.
  • Screen and treat non-cardiovascular comorbidity — diabetes, CKD, obesity, iron deficiency, anxiety and depression — and assess frailty.
  • Switch glucose-lowering drugs without proven cardiovascular safety to agents with proven benefit.
  • A multidisciplinary programme and exercise-based rehabilitation are core, not optional extras.

Section 16Key messages — STAMP on CKD

The guideline packages itself into an acronym: Screen, Triage and stage, Address risk, Modify cardiovascular management, Plan services.

Screen

  • Measure eGFR and albuminuria in every patient with cardiovascular disease — and look for cardiovascular disease in every patient with CKD.
  • Stage with eGFR plus a spot uACR, and confirm chronicity.
  • Re-test at least annually in diabetes, CKD and cardiovascular disease.

Triage and stage

  • Use the Kidney Failure Risk Equation in categories G3–5 to time nephrology referral.
  • Prefer cardiovascular risk scores that include eGFR and albuminuria.

Address risk

  • Intensive statin therapy, with or without ezetimibe, for CKD not on dialysis whatever the lipid level.
  • An ACE inhibitor or ARB plus an SGLT2 inhibitor for most patients with CKD.
  • Finerenone and semaglutide add further protection in type 2 diabetes with albuminuria.
  • SGLT2 inhibitors can be started at eGFR ≥ 20 and continued below it until kidney replacement therapy begins.

Modify cardiovascular management

  • Foundational HF therapy works as well in CKD; high absolute risk means large absolute benefit.
  • In coronary disease, start conservative, individualize revascularization, and shorten DAPT to limit bleeding.
  • In AF, existing stroke and bleeding scores fail below eGFR 30; DOACs beat vitamin K antagonists above it.
  • Permanent harm from contrast-associated AKI is rare — do not withhold or delay necessary imaging.

Plan services

  • Communication and patient education about CKD are part of the treatment.
  • Every clinician shares responsibility for slowing CKD and cutting cardiovascular risk.

Section 6Myocardial injury: acute and chronic

Injury is not infarction. Troponin above the 99th percentile only says myocytes released protein; the clinical context says why.

Acute myocardial injury

A rise and/or fall in cardiac troponin I or T with at least one value above the sex-specific 99th percentile upper reference limit.

Chronic myocardial injury

Two or more troponin values above the sex-specific 99th percentile when testing is done in a stable clinical setting. Confirmed when a cardiac or non-cardiac condition causing remodelling is identified; excluded when the rise is explained by analytical interference or reduced clearance.

Troponin rises chronically through three routes: more release from the myocardium, less clearance from the circulation, or analytical interference mimicking either. Halving the eGFR raises the concentration — but a raised troponin in kidney disease is never dismissed as clearance alone.

Section 7.2.1Primary myocardial infarction

Spontaneous onset, no other acute condition and no recent procedure to explain it. The criteria favour sensitivity — miss nothing coronary.

Diagnostic criteria

Likely — acute myocardial injury on troponin plus one or more of:

  • Symptoms consistent with acute myocardial ischaemia
  • New or presumed new ischaemic ECG changes
  • Development of pathological Q waves

Confirmed — one or more additional imaging features:

  • An acute coronary pathology: atherothrombosis, spontaneous coronary artery dissection, vasospasm or embolism; or restenosis, stent thrombosis or graft failure more than 30 days after revascularization
  • A new or presumed new regional wall motion abnormality and/or absence of viable myocardium in an ischaemic pattern
Where angiography and cardiac imaging are unavailable or inappropriate, the final diagnosis can still be made on biomarkers, ECG and clinical criteria — coded as unknown aetiology. Suspect dissection particularly in women under 50, in pregnancy and post-partum.

Section 7.2.2Secondary myocardial infarction

Ischaemia driven by another acute illness through oxygen supply–demand imbalance. Here the criteria favour specificity — call it infarction only when the label changes treatment.

Diagnostic criteria

Considered — acute myocardial injury with an alternative acute condition causing supply–demand mismatch, plus one or more of: ischaemic symptoms, new or presumed new ischaemic ECG changes, or pathological Q waves.

Likely — with known coronary artery disease, or strong clinical suspicion from the extent of ischaemia on ECG or the size of the troponin rise.

Confirmed — when imaging is feasible and appropriate, by one or more of:

  • Obstructive coronary artery disease — ≥ 70% epicardial stenosis on angiography, or ≥ 50% that is flow-limiting on physiological assessment — without acute coronary pathology
  • A new or presumed new regional wall motion abnormality or absence of viable myocardium in an ischaemic pattern
Demand rises with tachycardia and severe hypertension; supply falls with hypotension, hypoxia and anaemia — tachyarrhythmia is the commonest trigger and triggers often coexist. Most patients in this setting have neither obstructive disease nor new loss of viability, so the diagnosis is excluded rather than assumed. Sepsis and bleeding can also rupture plaque, so primary infarction stays on the differential.

Section 7.2.3Procedure-related myocardial infarction

A coronary complication of any cardiac procedure — percutaneous or surgical — within 30 days. No troponin multiple defines it; imaging does.

Diagnostic criteria

Considered — suspected coronary complication within 30 days of a cardiac procedure, with acute myocardial injury plus one or more of: ischaemic symptoms, new or presumed new ischaemic ECG changes, pathological Q waves, or unexplained sudden deterioration under sedation.

Likely immediately after the procedure when biomarkers have risen from the pre-procedural value and keep rising — as a guide, above 5 times the upper reference limit at 6 hours after intervention, or above 35 times at 24 hours after surgery.

Confirmed by one or more additional features — and by both when the complication arises during the procedure itself or the procedure was performed for an acute infarction:

  • Angiographic evidence of a complication: side branch occlusion, iatrogenic dissection, no or slow flow, recoil or early restenosis, stent thrombosis, graft failure, coronary impingement, embolism, or ostial occlusion during valve intervention
  • Cardiac imaging showing a new or presumed new regional wall motion abnormality or absence of viable myocardium in the territory of the artery involved
Some degree of acute injury is universal after these procedures, and its size depends on how long and how complex the case was — which is exactly why a single troponin threshold cannot work and imaging is required. ECG changes after cardiac surgery are common from pericarditis, pneumopericardium and electrolyte shifts, and Q waves can be transient.

Section 8–11Sudden death, silent infarction, unstable angina and MINOCA

Four situations that sit around the definition rather than inside it.

Death before the diagnosis is made

The old type 3 label is retired. Where infarction is the likely cause of death, apply the clinical classification from the setting or from post-mortem findings. Autopsy showing atherothrombosis, embolism, dissection, or stent or graft thrombosis beyond 30 days with necrosis in that artery's territory confirms primary infarction; an alternative acute condition raising demand or cutting supply points to secondary infarction; an acute coronary or graft occlusion within 30 days of a procedure confirms procedure-related infarction.

Silent or unrecognized infarction

Pathological Q waves on a routine ECG are not specific — confirm with imaging, preferably CMR with late gadolinium enhancement. About 1 in 5 people over 70 have unrecognized infarction on systematic CMR, and 3% of asymptomatic middle-aged adults. Many patients recall symptoms in retrospect, so "silent" can mislead. The finding matters: it carries an adverse prognosis and may change treatment.

Unstable angina

Still part of the acute coronary syndrome spectrum. Consider it when new rest or minimal-exertion angina, or a sudden intensification in chronic coronary syndrome, occurs and serial troponin excludes infarction. It has become less common as high-sensitivity assays reclassify many of these patients as infarction. Chronic troponin elevation is common in these patients and carries a worse prognosis.

MINOCA — myocardial injury with non-obstructive coronary arteries

A working diagnosis when a possible infarction turns out to have no stenosis ≥ 50% at angiography. The word changed from infarction to injury because most of these patients are eventually found to have myocarditis, Takotsubo syndrome, cardiomyopathy or a non-cardiac cause. Intravascular imaging can still reveal plaque erosion or rupture, mural thrombus or dissection, and functional testing can reveal spasm or microvascular dysfunction. CMR is best performed within 2 weeks; infarction is the final diagnosis in roughly a quarter of cases.

Section 14Cardiac troponin

The assay defines injury; the clinician defines infarction. Timing, baseline value and sex all change how a number should be read.

Interpreting troponin in possible infarction

  • Read it with the history, examination and 12-lead ECG — never alone.
  • Very early after symptom onset the rise may be undetectable even on a high-sensitivity assay: serial testing is necessary.
  • With recurrent or persistent ischaemic symptoms, repeat before excluding infarction.
  • In unselected patients tested without an indication, elevation is common in sepsis, kidney disease and stroke.
  • The 99th percentile is lower in women; sex-specific thresholds prevent under-diagnosis.
  • Age matters — both the prevalence of infarction and the prevalence of persistently raised values climb with it.
  • When a value is raised and infarction is not confirmed, serial measurement separates acute from chronic injury.
  • Suspect analytical interference when the value does not fit the clinical picture, or varies with no pattern on serial testing.

Why timing changes the delta

  • Within 3 hours of onset the absolute change is small; between 3 and 12 hours it is substantial.
  • Sampled at the 12–24 hour peak, serial values may barely move.
  • Presenting days later, the pattern can mimic chronic injury — and troponin can take weeks to normalize after infarction.
  • Fixed 1- or 2-hour intervals make delta criteria reliable; absolute deltas are assay-specific and cannot be carried between assays.
  • Near the 99th percentile use absolute deltas; with clearly high baselines relative change is more intuitive. At very high values a rise and fall may not need to be shown at all.
Accelerated pathways come in three shapes: single-sample rule-out, 0/1- or 0/2-hour serial testing with delta thresholds, and conventional 0/3-hour testing with a risk score. Their thresholds are for triage and often uniform rather than sex-specific — they do not define myocardial injury or confirm the final diagnosis. If a high-sensitivity assay is unavailable, a conventional troponin assay beats CK-MB; CK-MB by mass assay is the last resort.

Section 15Coronary and cardiac imaging

The diagnosis can be likely without imaging. Confirming it, and naming the mechanism, usually cannot be.

Invasive

  • Coronary angiography is the reference test and identifies the acute coronary pathology in primary infarction.
  • Intravascular imaging (OCT, IVUS) shows plaque rupture or erosion, mural thrombus, intramural haematoma of dissection, in-stent restenosis or thrombosis, and intimal bumping of spasm — about 14% of NSTEMI presentations have no clear culprit on angiography alone.
  • Provocation testing (acetylcholine, ergonovine) exposes epicardial or microvascular spasm; FFR, RFR or iFR grade whether a stenosis is functionally significant in suspected secondary infarction.

Non-invasive

  • Echocardiography, CMR, cardiac CT, SPECT and PET all contribute; CMR is the modality of choice when coronary arteries are non-obstructive.
  • CMR distinguishes infarction from myocarditis, Takotsubo syndrome and cardiomyopathy, and stages injury from oedema alone through necrosis, microvascular obstruction and haemorrhage.
  • CT coronary angiography is a useful alternative in lower-risk patients, in possible secondary infarction, or when revascularization is unlikely.
Historically, patients with supply–demand ischaemia rarely reached the catheter laboratory — and in a prospective study that imaged them systematically, 1 in 20 turned out to have primary infarction due to atherothrombosis.

Section 16Situations where the diagnosis is hardest

Recurrent events, heart failure, kidney disease, critical illness, surgery, structural intervention and cancer therapy — each distorts one of the diagnostic inputs.

Recurrent infarction

Within the first month, ECG, biomarkers and imaging may all still be abnormal from the index event. Troponin stays elevated for weeks, so a rising value is the signal. Angiography is often needed, and CMR can show whether there is more than one culprit. Classify by mechanism; there is no separate recurrence code.

Heart failure

Up to 90% of acute heart failure patients have troponin T above the reference limit. Measure troponin and record an ECG on admission, use serial values to separate acute from chronic injury, and compare imaging with previous studies since wall motion abnormalities are already common.

Chronic kidney disease

Troponin is raised in 70% of CKD patients with possible infarction (troponin T) versus 24% without. Half of raised values are injury without infarction — but half are not, so never blame clearance alone. The diagnostic pathway is unchanged; imaging matters more, and contrast risk is usually overstated relative to the benefit.

Critical illness

Infarction is among the most frequently missed diagnoses at post-mortem in the ICU. Sedation hides symptoms, ST elevation is common and less specific, and around 40% have injury with ischaemic ECG changes. The rise-and-fall pattern is the key discriminator, with cardiology input and angiography when the answer would change management.

Non-cardiac surgery

Most events occur within 2 days and up to two-thirds have no ischaemic symptoms. Peri-operative injury appears in about 1 in 8 high-risk operations; 1 in 20 of those is primary infarction. If troponin will be measured after surgery, a pre-operative value is essential — up to 40% of patients with cardiovascular disease start with a raised baseline.

Structural intervention and cardio-oncology

After TAVI, injury occurs in 66–100% of patients, so biomarkers alone cannot define an infarction — leaflet-related ostial obstruction (0.7%) and device embolism (1%) are the mechanisms to look for. In cancer therapy, anthracyclines and checkpoint inhibitors raise troponin through myocarditis and cardiotoxicity, while vasospasm and embolism are more common than in the general population.

The diagnosis is presumed in a patient with symptoms consistent with acute myocardial ischaemia plus one or more of: new ischaemic ECG changes, or development of pathological Q waves.
